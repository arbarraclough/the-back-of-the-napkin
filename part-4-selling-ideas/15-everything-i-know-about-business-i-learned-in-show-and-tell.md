# 15. Everything I Know About Business I Learned in Show & Tell

There are two big questions that Roam is often asked when it comes time to showing your ideas with pictures:

1. How can we best go about verbally describing a picture?
2. Are the pictures "bad" if they need any explanation at all?

## Everything I Know About Business I Learned in Show-and-Tell

Even though your team may have been involved in _creating_ the diagrams that are put in a slide deck, they may not know how to _talk_ about those diagrams.

- It's a different challenge because, other than the headline and labels on the coordinates, there's nothing to read.

> "No, I thought, the problem isn't with the pictures—the problem is in remembering that _show_ and _tell_ are two different words."

## Look, See, Imagine, Show: The Four Steps of Selling an Idea with a Picture

When you're giving your presentation, take your audience through the same journey that you took to make the pictures: _look_ at the landscape of information, _see_ the things in it that matter the most, _imagine_ what they mean, then _show_ the result. 

The only difference is that the information landscape is a plot you've already created, and you know exactly what you want to show.

Start by giving your audience the brief, e.g.:

> "As we all know, we've got a major problem to solve. Sales of SAM have flattened, and if we don't get sales back up in the next year, we stand to lose our top spot in the market. Our group believes that we've identified a solution, and we want to share it with you by taking you though this visual overview of our market."

Like the adage for structuring presentations: "Tell them what you will tell them, tell them, then tell them what you just told them."

### Start Looking Aloud

> "_Look:_ What's the picture about? What's included and what's not? What are the coordinates and dimensions?"

Start by giving a tour of your picture.

> "Our goal in creating this model was to build a baseline of our industry according to several critical factors... We believed that by looking at the business in this integrated way we would see our problem in a new light, potentially illuminating new and unexpected approaches to solving it."

Then, establish your horizontal axis.

> "First, we looked at what types of competitors we face, whether running on proprietary or open systems, which we have plotted here along the bottom."

Then, establish your vertical axis.

> "Next, we asked what kind of features each company's software offers, whether a full sprite or just a few. We plotted that here, going up the side."

Then, point out the extreme ends of the scales.

> "You see us up here in the lead with revenues last year of $25 million and the fullest feature set running on our proprietary platform, while you see MoneyFree way down here, with few features running on an open platform, and next to no revenue."

### Keep Seeing Aloud

> "_See:_ What are the three most important things that stand out? How do they interact? Is there a pattern emerging? Is there anything critical that we don't see?"

Now that you've established context by showing the _now_, you can start to draw the future on the same axis.

- With the same information presented as _now_ vs. _future_, it's easier to see why someone would care. This prompts questions.

### Continue by Imagining Aloud

> "_Imagine:_ How can we manipulate or take advantage of emerging patterns? Are there open opportunities? What is not visible here? Where have we seen this before?"

Imagining aloud means talking through the options that our picture presents and making the empty spaces come alive.

### Close by Showing Aloud

> "_Show:_ This is what we think it all means. Do you see the same things? This is what we think our options are. Do you agree?"

It's at this point that you can suggest which of the options you would go with and _why_.

You've made your argument and introduced more concepts quicker than would have been possible with words alone.

The diagrams made those concepts easy to see, understand, and remember.

They also provided a visual framework that prompted discussion.

## Sometimes a Pizza is Enough, Sometimes It's Not

> "If we're going to use a picture to sell, we have to be prepared to talk about it."

Answering [Question 2](#everything-i-know-about-business-i-learned-in-show-and-tell), "all good pictures do _not_ need to be self-explanatory, but they do need to be _explainable_."

The point of any picture/diagram isn't to replace _all_ the words.

It's used to replace the words that are more effectively conveyed, understood, and remembered visually.

Most day-to-day meetings are "pizza meetings", meaning that the goal is to "feed" everyone with information that is simple, easy-, and quick-to-digest.

Some meetings are different.

They're more akin to "three-course dinners" where attendees expect to be impressed, learn something they didn't know, and see something they've never seen before.

- In these sorts of meetings, it's **normal** that the diagrams/pictures will need a lot of explanation.
  They convey substantial insight, invite interesting conversations, and support important decision making.
