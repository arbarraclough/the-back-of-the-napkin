# 11. Where Is Our Business?

> **"Framework 3: To show a where problem, use a map."**

> "Maps make all kinds of ideas about the spatial relationships of objects unexpectedly clear."

Maps are the most flexible of all six frameworks, meaning that various kinds of maps may not look that much alike.

## Maps: General Rules of Thumb

1. **Everything has a geography.**

    Anything that is built up from multiple unique components can be mapped.

2. **North is a state of mind.**

    Rather than using North/South/East/West as your coordinate system, you can make maps of anything using pairs of opposites.

3. **Look beyond the obvious hierarchy.**

    Imagine a traditional hierarchical organisation chart. They're good for showing the official chain of command, but they don't capture the less-obvious-but-more-powerful political connections needed to get things done. This information is harder to collect, but is often worth it.

## Case Study: SAX Inc.

### Concept Map

One type of map you can create is somewhere between a concept model and a treasure map that shows the structure of a company.

![Structure Map](images/structure-map.png)

The diagram of a client's company shows:

- The different divisions within the company (symbols),
- Their different areas of operation (dotted lines),
- The dependencies between different departments (solid "roads"),
- Which departments purchase our software (red X's).

This divisional structure of a client helps to give you a useful overview, but it isn't enough.

You need to be able to see the hierarchical connections between those domains, i.e., who decides what and who influences whom.

### Organisation Chart

Another "map" that's commonly found in businesses is the organisation chart.

If you ask somebody to sketch out how their company works, the first picture they'd typically draw is this hierarchical, top-down organisation chart.

![Organisation Chart](images/organisation-chart.png)

However, often the most insightful thing about this chart is what it _doesn't show_, e.g., the human connections that make things work outside of the hierarchy.

![Relationship Chart](images/relationship-chart.png)

In this example, because of Jason's direct connection with every level across the business, he influences their decision-making, whether directly or indirectly, so it makes sense to figure out what he thinks makes a good/bad product.

![Influence Chart](images/influence-chart.png)

Now, rather than purely focusing on the departments/organisation chart, you can model the aspects of a product different people want, and what their connection to each other is.

- A Venn diagram is exactly this because, conceptually, it's a map used to show the overlap between any kinds of object.

![Venn Diagram](images/venn-diagram.png)

### Concept Model

When modelling a product, e.g., a software system, you can use a **concept model** which breaks things down into components.

- Each of these components can then be broken down further into roles/responsibilities.
- These components can be linked together, showing their dependency on one another.

![Concept Model](images/concept-model.png)
